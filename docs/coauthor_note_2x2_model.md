# Coauthor Note — Reading of the 2×2 Competitive Solicitation Model

*Working note responding to the model sketch. Companion to the literature review
("Reviews and Ratings") and the empirical proposal ("The Silence of the Satisfied").*

## Reading of the sketch

There are two firms and two ways of getting reviews. Strategy A (Strategy 1) is
firm-solicited; Strategy B (Strategy 2) is consumer-driven. The two off-diagonal
cells — where the firms make different choices — are shaded, so those are taken as
the outcomes of interest: one firm soliciting while its rival relies on organic
reviews. (Confirm the shading means this.)

If so, it is a better frame for a model than the one originally proposed, and a
paper already in the review suggests the shaded cells could be where firms actually
end up.

## Why the two firms might choose differently

Sun (2012) shows that more spread in ratings raises demand only when the average
rating is low, because a spread-out distribution reads as a niche product some
people love. Soliciting reviews narrows that spread by pulling in customers with
middling experiences. So:

- A **strong firm** wants to solicit — that gives it a tight distribution around a
  high average.
- A **weaker firm** may do better leaving reviews to consumers, keeping the spread
  that makes it look niche.

The choice of review strategy then sorts firms by quality, which lands in exactly
the shaded cells.

## How it ties to Google and Yelp

The two platforms change which cells are available at all. On Google, firms can pick
any of the four. Yelp forbids asking for reviews, so every firm is stuck with
consumer-driven reviews in the bottom-right cell, and the mixed outcomes cannot
happen. Platform policy then does more than act as a treatment — it decides which
game firms get to play. That gives the field comparison a clear prediction: the
model tells you what should differ between a platform where firms can split and one
where they cannot.

## Choices the coauthor would need to settle first

- **Whether the firms differ in quality or in taste.** Quality differences give the
  sorting story above. Taste differences give a positioning story where one firm
  goes broad and the other niche.
- **What consumers make of the ratings.** De Langhe et al. suggest people take
  ratings at face value. If they do not adjust for how reviews were collected,
  soliciting is a straight advantage for strong firms. If they do adjust,
  soliciting becomes a signal and can backfire.
- **Whether soliciting costs the firm anything, and whether consumers can see that
  it happened.** If a solicited distribution looks the same as an organic one to
  consumers, there is no signal — only a different displayed rating.

## Where the empirical/experimental part comes in

- **Empirically:** test whether Google businesses really split into the two
  strategies and whether the split tracks quality. Soliciting leaves traces, like
  bursts of reviews from people who have never reviewed anything before.
- **Experimentally:** show participants two competing businesses, one with a tight,
  solicited-looking distribution and one with a polarized one. Vary their average
  ratings and see which gets chosen. This checks the assumption about consumer
  inference that the model depends on.
