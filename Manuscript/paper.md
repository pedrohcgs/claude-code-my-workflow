# Surfacing Data Value: What Makes Data Valuable to Hybrid Organizations under the Economies of Worth Lens — A Q Methodology Study

## 1. Introduction

Data has become a strategic asset for organizations across every sector, yet how its value should be understood, and by whom, remains unsettled. Existing approaches span cost-based, income-based, and market-based methods ([@coyleManley2024]; [@deloitte2024]), computational approaches that estimate the contribution of individual data points to a model's performance ([@ghorbaniZou2019]), with applied extensions to revenue-sharing among data-network participants ([@badewitz2020]), and evidence that even individuals' own valuations of their data resist a single economic calculus — Spiekermann and Korunovska [-@spiekermannKorunovska2017] find that consciousness of data as a tradable asset, not privacy concern, is the strongest driver of what people demand to share it — yet no consensus has emerged on which methods fit the diverse perspectives, characteristics, and contexts that make data valuable. Statistical agencies have only just begun to converge on how data should be recognized as an asset at all. In 2025, the System of National Accounts recognized data itself, as distinct from the databases that store it, as a produced asset for the first time, with a companion Eurostat handbook issued the same year to guide implementation ([@sna2025]; [@eurostat2025]). This convergence, decades after data became central to economic activity, illustrates how unsettled data valuation remains, even before asking whose worth counts.

A growing body of work treats data's value as a relational, socio-technically constructed property rather than a fixed attribute of the data itself, dependent on who seeks to use data, for what purpose, and through what process. Alaimo, Kallinikos, and Aaltonen [-@alaimoKallinikosAaltonen2020] show that data acquires value only as it is worked on through organizational processes of collection, structuring, and analysis, and Nabben [-@nabben2025] argues that value is co-produced through socio-technical relationships mediated by the infrastructures through which data moves. The World Economic Forum [-@wef2021] draws the practical consequence, arguing that articulating data's value requires moving beyond transactional monetization to account for how ecosystem stakeholders experience it.

This paper follows Castro Fernandez's [-@castroFernandez2025] effort to give data's value a rigorous treatment for articulation. His account separates data, whose value is objective, from documents, whose value is subjective and depends on the task a user puts them to. That separation leaves the subjective, use-dependent side of value identified but not yet developed. This paper takes up that space, extending it to provide a rigorous vocabulary for the plural grounds in which actors judge data's use to be valuable. Boltanski and Thévenot's [-@boltanskiThevenot2006] economies of worth supplies such vocabulary. This includes the concept of investments of form, which are durable, standardized articulations, such as prices, statistics, and certifications, that make a claim about worth defensible, hence offering a way to give grounds other than monetization an articulated robustness to express data's value.

This need is especially acute for hybrid organizations, which combine commercial and social-mission logics and must reconcile competing institutional demands while sustaining both financial viability and mission legitimacy ([@battilanaLee2014]). As these organizations pursue data-sharing collaborations and adopt data science and AI tools, decisions about how the resulting value should be recognized and distributed require a vocabulary that does not yet exist in developed form. Ammann and Hess [-@ammannHess2025] find value capture in decentralized data ecosystems to be a genuinely open, contested problem, and recommend that practitioners develop value-capture principles which need not be monetary. Nienstedt and Trenz [-@nienstedtTrenz2025] similarly identify, and begin to address, the gap in what determines an organization's capacity to create value from shared data and what ensures its effective appropriation. Absent a developed vocabulary, mission-oriented considerations risk being systematically outcompeted in data-sharing decisions, not because they are less valid or legitimate worth, but for lack of comparably robust terms in which to state and defend them.

## 2. Research Gap

The literature above establishes that data's value is plural and contested, and that this plurality carries real costs when organizations try to create and distribute value from data. However, it has not explained why some worth claims prevail over others in practice. Economies of worth supplies a framework equipped to do so, treating value as legitimately contested across distinct orders such as market, industrial, and civic rather than as a single scale. Thévenot's [-@thevenot1984] investments of form add a mechanism for this explanation: certain justifications may dominate not because they are more valid, but because they are backed by more developed proof devices, such as prices, statistics, and certifications, that make a claim easier to defend in a dispute. Neither the framework nor testing this mechanism has yet been applied to data value.

This gives the paper two theoretical contributions and a methodological one. First, it extends economies of worth and investments of form to a domain where their explanatory power has not yet been tested. For instance, Stark [-@stark2009] on the productive dissonance of multiple worth-principles within organizations, Sharon [-@sharon2018] on the plurality of orders of worth at stake in digital health, and Siffels [-@siffels2020] on a contact-tracing-app debate (further applications to be added; Fourcade [-@fourcade2011], on competing valuation regimes for environmental damage, belongs to the wider valuation-studies literature rather than an orders-of-worth application specifically). Within hybrid organizations specifically, institutional logics theory explains how organizations structurally arrange competing logics — Pache and Santos [-@pacheSantos2013] identify selective coupling as one such arrangement, Besharov and Smith [-@besharovSmith2014] show how logic compatibility and centrality shape the consequences of multiplicity, and even the framework's most recent microfoundational turn, Dorobat, McCaffrey, and Topan's [-@dorobatMcCaffreyTopan2024] judgment-based account of hybridity, works at the level of an individual's own trade-off between monetary and psychic profit — but none of this work explains why, in a specific dispute over data's value, one logic's claims prove easier to defend than another's. That is the question investments of form answers and institutional logics, even at its most current, does not ask. Commensuration theory offers a different rival mechanism: Espeland and Stevens [-@espelandStevens1998] show how a common metric can absorb and eliminate rival value dimensions by reducing them to one scale, and Van Bommel, Rasche, and Spicer [-@vanBommelRascheSpicer2023] document exactly this outcome in sustainability reporting, where commensuration into a single "value" metric crowds out the moral grounds sustainability once rested on. Economies of worth describes the opposite outcome for data value — orders that remain plural and mutually irreducible, some simply better equipped to defend themselves than others — which is precisely what this paper's expected asymmetry finding requires: civic and green worth are expected to survive as legitimate but under-instrumented, not crowded out or absorbed into a market metric. Service-dominant logic, developed in §3a, supplies the premise that value is plural and beneficiary-determined but offers no vocabulary for how actors justify or contest a particular valuation against a rival one; economies of worth picks up exactly where that silence begins. Second, it pairs economies of worth with Q methodology, a technique long used to surface plural viewpoints in cultural and ecological valuation, where non-market values are similarly difficult to reduce to a single scale ([@zabalaSandbrookMukherjee2018]; [@pikeEtAl2015]), but not yet combined with economies of worth as an analytical lens. This combination gives the qualitative structure of orders of worth an empirically operationalized, factor-analytic counterpart, extending Q methodology's own domain of application.

For practitioners, there is a parallel gap. Hybrid organizations and their commercial data-sharing counterparts currently lack a vocabulary for articulating and defending non-monetary grounds of data's worth in negotiations over data sharing, which leaves mission-oriented considerations vulnerable to being outcompeted regardless of their merit. Identifying the orders of worth actors actually draw on, and where these are comparatively under-instrumented, offers organizations a basis for auditing their own data decisions and supports the fairer, and not necessarily only monetary value capture principles that Ammann and Hess [-@ammannHess2025] call for.

The gap is therefore twofold: no study has produced an actor-grounded account of the specific orders of worth hybrid-organization actors invoke — to justify data's value, and none has examined why some of these justifications come to dominate in practice. We address this gap by asking the following main research question:

*How do hybrid-organization actors justify what makes data valuable, and which repertoires of justification — including ones that already combine competing orders of worth — do they draw on when negotiating how that value is created and distributed?*

More specifically:

1. What distinct viewpoints on data value can be identified among these actors, and which orders of worth, or compromises between orders, characterize each?
2. Which viewpoints on data value combine multiple orders of worth, and what instruments or justifications do participants invoke to hold them together?
3. On which aspects of data value do viewpoints converge, and on which do they diverge — and how does that convergence map onto the distinction between value creation and value capture?
4. Why do some orders of worth prove easier to defend than others in disputes over data's value, and does this asymmetry trace to differences in each order's investments of form? <!-- provisional -- revisit before final draft; may be dropped or folded into RQ2/3 -->

## 3. Conceptual Background

The study is organized around three complementary literature streams.

### 3a. The Data Value Literature

Data value literature spans several disciplines and angles of inquiry, from economic and computational approaches to data's application within business organizations ([@coyleManley2024]; [@kvalvik2026]). A recurring distinction within this literature separates data's intrinsic value from its usage value, the latter more heavily represented, understood as value realized through data's application across different fields, contexts, and purposes. Structured accounts such as Castro Fernandez's [-@castroFernandez2025] argue that progress on generating value from data depends on rigorous definitions of what value is and what data is in the first place, distinguishing data itself — whose value is objective, proportional to the precision of what it represents — from documents, whose value depends on the task a user puts them to. Much of this literature concentrates on the properties that make data monetizable directly or useful indirectly through process improvement. Coyle and Manley's [-@coyleManley2024] review maps and critiques the resulting landscape into cost-based, income-based, and market-based methods, finding no consensus on which should be applied — with data's further potential remaining shaped by the same monetary factors and by the economic-level outputs the 2025 System of National Accounts now recognizes by treating data itself, distinct from the databases that store it, as a produced asset for the first time ([@sna2025]; [@eurostat2025]). Mohan, Bharathy, and Jalan's [-@mohanBharathyJalan2026] targeted review of enterprise data valuation extends this line further, cataloguing approaches from customer-lifetime-value and shareholder-value methods to cooperative-game-theoretic and machine-learning-based estimators — a proliferation of techniques that itself signals the absence of a settled standard.

Alongside this, a stream of literature treats data's value as contextual, relational, and use-dependent rather than a property waiting to be measured. This stream traces to marketing's service-dominant-logic tradition, where Vargo and Lusch [-@vargoLusch2004; -@vargoLusch2008] relocate value away from goods with value embedded in them and toward service provision, in which value is co-created between provider and beneficiary rather than delivered ready-made. Grönroos [-@gronroos2011] sharpens the claim further: firms do not create value directly but only facilitate it, since value-in-use is generated by the customer at the moment and in the context of use. Hunke, Heinz, and Satzger [-@hunkeHeinzSatzger2021] bring this logic to data specifically, defining analytics-based services that realize customer value by applying data to a task, organized into four archetypal service types built around that task-application logic. Xu, Indulska, Asadi Someh, and Shanks [-@xuEtAl2024] formalize the resulting plurality directly, distinguishing four roles data can play — as a tool, as a commodity, as a practice, and as algorithmic intelligence — each generating value along a different, empirically distinguishable pathway. Kvalvik, Misra, Mishra, and Dash [-@kvalvik2026] complement this with a systematic review of the economic traits that set data apart from conventional commodities — non-rivalry, non-excludability, and scalability — that together resist conventional valuation frameworks. Wixom, Beath, and Owens [-@wixomBeathOwens2023] mark the resulting distinction plainly: creating value from data and monetizing that value are not the same accomplishment, since data must still be converted into improved processes, product features, or sold information before it becomes a financial outcome. Together, these sources locate precisely the space Castro Fernandez [-@castroFernandez2025] leaves open when he separates data's objective value from the task-dependent judgment a document's use requires. What remains missing is a vocabulary rigorous enough to hold that judgment to the same standard he applies to data's objective value, which is the role economies of worth is positioned to play.

### 3b. Economies of Worth

The second stream supplies the study's analytical lens. Boltanski and Thévenot's [-@boltanskiThevenot2006] polity model holds that people justify decisions and resolve disputes over what is good by drawing on a small set of shared orders of worth, legitimate and mutually irreducible value systems including the market, industrial, civic, domestic, inspired, and fame orders, later extended with a green order valuing ecological worth ([@lafayeThevenot1993]; [@thevenotMoodyLafaye2000]) and a project or connexionist order valuing flexibility and network position ([@boltanskiChiapello2005]). As Trevisan and Mouritsen [-@trevisanMouritsen2023] set out, worth is established through tests that attribute a state of worth to people and objects in practice, and disputes are resolved through prioritization, in which one order is ordered above the others, or through compromise, in which actors treat ambiguous or composite objects as satisfying several orders simultaneously without settling which one dominates. Such compromises remain inherently fragile, since the objects gathered within them continue to belong to their order of origin and can be reopened by renewed critique. These orders also function, more simply, as a shared grammar of justification — a finite set of registers actors can invoke, some of which stabilize into durable, ready-to-hand combinations of more than one order, distinct from a fresh compromise negotiated within a single live dispute. This study works at the level of that grammar: which registers hybrid-organization actors draw on, and which combinations they already hold as coherent, when justifying data's value. This apparatus suits hybrid organizations well, since they must routinely hold commercial and mission-driven considerations together without collapsing one into the other. Economies of worth is positioned here as complementary to institutional logics theory rather than as a competing grand theory of hybridity: it supplies the microfoundational, justification-level account of situated disputes and compromises that institutional logics, with its focus on relatively stable field-level vocabularies, leaves underdeveloped ([@cloutierLangley2013]).

### 3c. Value Creation and Value Capture

The third pillar draws on the strategy literature distinguishing value creation from value capture. Bowman and Ambrosini [-@bowmanAmbrosini2000] provide the foundational distinction: value creation enlarges the total value available in an exchange, while value capture, or appropriation, determines who gets what share of it, a division they locate in the bargaining power between the parties involved. Applied to data ecosystems, Ammann and Hess [-@ammannHess2025] show decentralized data-sharing arrangements can enlarge value creation while making value capture harder and more contested, producing distinct business-model types such as selling, donating, and bartering data. Nienstedt and Trenz [-@nienstedtTrenz2025] extend this by showing the capacity to create value and the strategies actors use to appropriate it are interdependent, since insufficient means of appropriation remove an organization's incentive to keep creating value. This pillar is proposed as the third conceptual anchor because it supplies the precise empirical dyad, creation and capture, around which the paper's research questions and Q-set are organized, bridging the multi-stakeholder framing of the data-value literature and the justificatory vocabulary of economies of worth with a concrete mechanism through which value disputes arise and are settled, or left unsettled.

## 4. Methodology

The study uses Q methodology, a technique built for surfacing shared subjectivity rather than testing predefined hypotheses. Participants sort the statements under a stated condition of instruction: how they would justify the way value from data is created or distributed in a data-sharing negotiation, so that agreement or disagreement reflects a justificatory position on value creation and distribution specifically, not a free-floating belief about data value in the abstract. Consistent with Q methodology's own account of operant subjectivity — a viewpoint articulable at a given moment rather than a fixed trait [-@wattsStenner2012] — each resulting factor is interpreted as a justificatory repertoire available to actors in data-sharing negotiations, not as a stable type of actor. A concourse of approximately one hundred value statements is developed through a hybrid technique standard in Q methodology: an initial set drawn from the literature reviewed above, structured as a matrix crossing the orders of worth with the value-creation and value-capture distinction, supplemented by naturalistic material from twelve semi-structured expert interviews — sampled from telecommunications and banking (2), European data-space initiatives (3), organizations representing AI-affected participants in the UK (2), and NGO data partnerships (1), with the remaining interviewees drawn from data cooperatives or unions and an environmental-sector perspective [TODO: finalize remaining expert interviewees] — conducted specifically to fill matrix cells the literature-derived statements do not adequately cover. From this concourse, a Q-set of forty statements is distilled through empirical piloting, to test clarity and comprehensibility, and independent double-coding of each candidate statement against the matrix by both co-authors, with disagreements resolved by discussion, so the theoretical structure enters at the coding stage rather than being assigned by a single researcher's judgment alone. This Q-set is administered to a purposively heterogeneous P-set of thirty participants. Eligibility is defined by role rather than by geography: participants hold a role with some decision-making or advisory stake in how their organization shares, governs, or monetizes data, spanning data or technology leadership, legal or data-protection compliance, partnership or business-development roles that negotiate data-sharing arrangements, executive leadership, and, within hybrid and social-economy organizations specifically, mission or programme-delivery leads representing the social-mission side of data decisions. Within this frame, the sample is drawn roughly two-thirds from hybrid and social-economy organizations and one-third from commercial organizations, the latter serving as an explicit contrast group rather than a focal population. Social-economy organizations — cooperatives, mutuals, associations — are treated as hybrid in this sampling frame because they combine commercial data-sharing activity with a social mission, consistent with Battilana and Lee's [-@battilanaLee2014] definition. The 2:1 ratio follows Q methodology's own logic for subgroup size: roughly ten participants comfortably exceeds the conventional four-to-eight-participant floor needed for a distinct viewpoint to emerge within the commercial contrast group, while the remaining twenty preserve room for the plural viewpoints expected within the focal hybrid and social-economy population. Recruitment draws on multiple channels rather than a single network, so the sample is not confined to one country: contacts from the Torino Social Impact ecosystem (at least ten), a subset — not all — of the twelve concourse-building expert interviewees re-invited to also complete a Q-sort (an overlap disclosed here rather than left implicit), and further contacts recruited against the eligibility criterion above. Approximately forty contacts are invited to reach the target of thirty completed sorts, allowing for attrition. Each participant completes a forced Q-sort, ranking every statement from most agree to most disagree against a quasi-normal forced-distribution grid [TODO: finalize exact grid range], a procedure that sharpens critical engagement and reduces the tendency toward neutral responses common in conventional surveys. Immediately following the sort, each participant completes a post-sort interview, explaining their placement of the most-agree and most-disagree statements and, specifically, which instruments they draw on to justify that placement. This qualitative layer, together with a document analysis of the proof devices actually used in participating organizations' data-sharing agreements and reporting, supplies the evidence needed to assess whether some orders of worth are more heavily instrumented than others — a question the Q-sort ranking alone cannot answer. By-person factor analysis — principal components extraction with varimax rotation, retaining factors with eigenvalues greater than 1 that prove substantively interpretable, with loadings treated as significant above approximately 0.41 (2.58 × 1/√40), conducted in KADE — identifies a small number of empirically distinct viewpoints, each interpreted through its most significant statements, participant characteristics, and the post-sort interview data, and cross-checked for statements shared across viewpoints as well as statements that sharply distinguish one viewpoint from another.

## 5. Expected Results

The study is expected to yield a small number of empirically distinct, named viewpoints on data value, each associated with a dominant order of worth or a candidate compromise between orders, though this alignment is anticipated rather than assumed in advance. A factor that cuts across the order-of-worth structure, or that clusters around a register the framework does not name, would count as evidence against orders of worth fitting data value as well as expected. Illustrative examples of these viewpoints:

- An inspired, innovation-oriented viewpoint treating data's worth as inseparable from creative reuse
- A civic, redistributive viewpoint treating worth as inseparable from who benefits and who bears risk
- A market-oriented viewpoint treating worth chiefly as a matter of price and exchange

Rather than a flat typology, results are to derive consensus and divisive statements about the potential coexistence or not of various orders or worth. Consensus is expected to concentrate around value-creation statements, on which most viewpoints agree that data becomes valuable only once collected, structured, and used, while the sharpest distinguishing statements should concentrate around value-capture and appropriation, that is, around who should own, profit from, or bear the cost of data. A second expected finding, drawn from the post-sort interviews and the accompanying document analysis rather than from the Q-sort ranking alone, is an asymmetry of investments of form: market and industrial orders are expected to be backed by heavy-duty, standardized proof devices such as prices and key performance indicators, making them easier to defend in a dispute, whereas civic, domestic, inspired, and green orders remain comparatively weakly instrumented for the data-sharing test specifically — despite civic and green worth having well-developed proof devices in other domains (certifications, ESG reporting) that have not yet migrated into data-sharing practice. The same document analysis is expected to surface where such instruments already exist for these weaker orders and where they remain to be built, a gap taken up directly in the design-science follow-up (§6.3).

These results are expected to deliver contributions to theory and practice. **Theoretically**, the study operationalizes the microfoundations of value empirically, complementing institutional logics theory at the justification level ([@cloutierLangley2013]). It also complements Nabben's [-@nabben2025] relational concept of value by identifying the justificatory repertoires actors bring to socio-technically mediated data relations, rather than converting that relational account directly into a typology of individual viewpoints. And it explains, through the instrumentation-asymmetry finding, not merely that justifications differ but why some come to dominate data-value disputes. **For practice**, the study supplies a stakeholder vocabulary answering the World Economic Forum's [-@wef2021] call to understand how different stakeholders experience value, lays groundwork for the fair, not-necessarily-monetary value-capture principles that Ammann and Hess [-@ammannHess2025] call for, and offers hybrid organizations a diagnostic tool for auditing which orders of worth are represented, and structurally outcompeted, in their own data decisions.

## 6. Future Research

This paper is designed to seed potential follow-up studies:

1. The first takes the typology of value viewpoints identified here as the input set for a discrete choice experiment quantifying how organizations weigh and trade off value dimensions in data-sharing decisions.
2. The second uses the identified orders of worth and compromises as the analytic lens for case studies of real data-sharing negotiations, examining how actors deploy and test justifications from different orders in interaction and what negotiation practices allow weakly-instrumented orders to be voiced and respected alongside heavily-instrumented ones.
3. A third, further-out line of work takes up the instrumentation-asymmetry finding through design science research, developing instruments that extend investments of form to currently under-equipped orders, particularly civic, domestic, connection, and green worth, so these can be materialized and defended in investment and data-sharing decisions in the social economy without collapsing plural worth into a single metric.
